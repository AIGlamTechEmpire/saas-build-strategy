# DAY 3 — The Hair Problem

**Format:** Carousel, 7 slides
**Teaching point:** "Textured hair is hard to draw" is a practice problem, not
a fact.
**CTA:** Soft — follow, comment

## Slide copy

**SLIDE 1 — Hook**
> ## "Coily hair is too hard to draw."
> No. It's just less practiced.

**SLIDE 2 — Tension**
> ## Artists draw what they've drawn before.
> Straight hair gets thousands of reps. Coils get almost none.
> That's the whole gap.

**SLIDE 3 — Value**
> ## Coils aren't lines. They're shapes.
> Stop drawing individual strands. Draw the clumps —
> the big forms the curls group into.

**SLIDE 4 — Value**
> ## Light sits differently on a coil.
> It breaks into scattered highlights instead of one long shine.
> Scattered is correct. One glossy stripe is the mistake.

**SLIDE 5 — Value**
> ## The silhouette is the identity.
> Aoi's hair is half her recognizable shape.
> Flatten it and you've drawn somebody else.

**SLIDE 6 — Payoff**
> ## It was never harder.
> It was just never prioritized. That changes with who's holding the pen.

**SLIDE 7 — CTA**
> ## Tell us the character who got it right.
> Drop a name in the comments.
> **@yourhandle**

## Instagram caption

"Textured hair is hard to draw."

You hear it constantly, and it's backwards. Artists get good at what they draw
repeatedly. Straight hair gets thousands of reps in every tutorial, every
reference pack, every how-to-draw book. Coils get almost none. That's not
difficulty. That's practice, distributed unevenly.

Here's what actually changes when you learn it:

**Draw clumps, not strands.** Coils group into larger forms. Chasing individual
hairs gets you noise.

**Let the light scatter.** A coil breaks highlights into many small catches
instead of one long shine. If you render a single glossy stripe down the middle,
you've drawn a wig.

**Protect the silhouette.** Aoi's hair is half her recognizable shape. Flatten
it and she stops being herself. That's true for any character.

Our students learn this in Week 2, when they design their own character's face
and hair. A lot of them design their own texture for the first time and you can
watch it land.

Who's a character that got the hair right? Comments.

## Facebook rewrite

# DAY 4 — Anime Is a Medium, Not a Genre

**Format:** Reel / single image
**Teaching point:** The format is far bigger than the stereotype.
**CTA:** Follow

## Reel script

**Length:** 35 seconds

**Shot 1 (0–6s)** — Aoi mid-frame, arms folded, direct address.
> "Somebody's going to tell you anime is cartoons for kids. They're describing
> a format like it's a genre."

**Shot 2 (7–16s)** — Slow orbit around her, city lights streaking.
> "Anime is a way of making something, not a thing it's about. There are anime
> sports dramas. Political thrillers. Cooking shows. Quiet stories where
> nothing explodes at all."

**Shot 3 (17–27s)** — Push in to a medium close.
> "Live action isn't a genre. Neither is animation. The camera doesn't decide

**Shot 4 (28–35s)** — She gestures out at the city.
> "Which means the story you want to tell already fits. Go build it."

**On-screen text:** "anime = a MEDIUM" / "not a genre" / "your story already fits"

**Generation prompt:** locked character block, then —


## Instagram caption

Anime is not a genre. It's a medium.

That distinction sounds academic until you watch it change what a kid thinks
they're allowed to make.

Genre is what a story is about — horror, romance, mystery. Medium is how it
gets made. Anime is a way of building something, and it holds every genre there
is. Sports dramas. Political thrillers. Slow quiet stories about a family
running a restaurant. Horror. Comedy. Historical fiction.

Nobody calls live action a genre. Same logic.

Why it matters: when a young creator thinks anime means one specific kind of
story, they assume their idea doesn't qualify. It does. The story they actually
want to tell already fits, because the format was never the limit.

Our Week 4 is entirely this — students write their own story, in whatever genre
they actually care about.

## Facebook rewrite

A quick reframe for parents.

Anime is a medium, not a genre — a way of making something rather than a thing
it's about. It covers sports stories, historical drama, mystery, comedy, and
quiet family stories, the same way live action does.

This matters more than it sounds. When a young person thinks anime only means
one kind of story, they assume their own ideas don't belong. Teaching them the
difference opens up what they think they're allowed to create.

In week four of our program, students write their own original story in whatever
genre they genuinely care about. Many of them are surprised that's an option.

# DAY 5 — Character Design 101

**Format:** Carousel, 7 slides
**Teaching point:** The three-part test every good character passes.
**CTA:** Soft — save the post

## Slide copy

**SLIDE 1 — Hook**
> ## Three tests. Every character you love passes all three.

**SLIDE 2 — Tension**
> ## Most first characters fail on the same thing.
> Too many details, no clear shape.

**SLIDE 3 — Value**
> ## Test one: the silhouette.
> Fill your character in solid black. Still recognizable?
> If not, the shape isn't doing enough work yet.

**SLIDE 4 — Value**
> ## Test two: the palette.
> Three to five colors, and one of them dominates.
> A character with fifteen colors reads as noise.

**SLIDE 5 — Value**
> ## Test three: the motif.
> One shape or symbol, repeated. Aoi has hearts —
> earrings, belt, chain, boots. Repetition builds recognition.

**SLIDE 6 — Payoff**
> ## Shape, palette, motif.
> Get those three right and the details take care of themselves.

**SLIDE 7 — CTA**
> ## Save this and run the test on your own character.
> Then post it and tag us.
> **@yourhandle**

## Instagram caption

Three tests. Run your character through all three.

**1. The silhouette test.** Fill your character in solid black. Can you still
tell who it is? Aoi passes on four things — the coil halo of her hair, the
popped collar, the long coat tail, the straight column of her leggings and
boots. If your character in black is just a person-shaped blob, the shape isn't
working yet.

**2. The palette test.** Three to five colors, one dominant. Aoi is violet, with
black and a little magenta. That's it. Beginners tend to add every color they
like, and the character stops reading at a distance.

**3. The motif test.** One shape, repeated. Aoi has hearts — earrings, hair
pins, collar studs, belt, chain, thigh rings, boots. You register it before you
consciously notice it, and that's exactly the point. Repetition is how a
character becomes memorable instead of just nice-looking.

Most first characters fail on test one. They pile on details before the shape is
solid. Shape first, always.

Save this. Run it on your own character. Then show us.

## Facebook rewrite

If your young person is designing characters at home, here's a simple framework
they can use tonight.

**Silhouette:** fill the character in solid black — can you still recognize
them? Good design reads as a shape before it reads as detail.

**Palette:** three to five colors, one dominant. More than that and the
character stops reading clearly.

**Motif:** one shape repeated throughout. It's what makes a character memorable
rather than just well-drawn.

These are the same fundamentals used in professional character design, and
they're taught in week two of our program. Show this to your artist — it will
change the next thing they draw.
