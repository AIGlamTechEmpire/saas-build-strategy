# DAY 6 — Inspired vs. Copied

**Format:** Reel / single image
**Teaching point:** The originality principle, in language a twelve-year-old can
use.
**CTA:** Soft

## Reel script

**Length:** 40 seconds

**Shot 1 (0–7s)** — Aoi close, direct address.
> "There's a line between inspired and copied, and most people never get taught
> where it is."

**Shot 2 (8–18s)** — Medium, she gestures.
> "Copied is taking the character. The name, the face, the powers, the outfit.
> Change the hair color and it's still theirs."

**Shot 3 (19–30s)** — Slow push in.
> "Inspired is taking the *question*. Somebody built a story about a kid
> carrying a weight they didn't choose. That question is yours to answer too.
> Your answer just has to be yours."

**Shot 4 (31–40s)** — She turns toward the city.
> "Be inspired. Don't copy. Build your own universe."

**On-screen text:** "copied = the character" / "inspired = the question" /
"build your own"

**Generation prompt:** locked character block, then —
```
Composition: close-up direct address to camera, then pulling to a medium as she
gestures with an open hand, finishing with her turning to look out over the neon
skyline. Calm authoritative expression throughout. Violet rim light, city bokeh.
```

## Instagram caption

Be inspired. Don't copy. Build your own universe.

That's the first rule in our studio, and it's the one that takes the longest to
teach — because the line is genuinely blurry when nobody's explained it.

**Copied** is taking the character. The name, the face, the powers, the outfit,
the world. Recoloring the hair and changing one letter in the name does not make
it yours. Every young artist tries this once. It's a normal step, not a moral
failing, and it's also not a portfolio.

**Inspired** is taking the *question*. A creator built a story around a kid
carrying a responsibility they never asked for. That question belongs to
everybody — it's the human part. Your answer has to be your own: your character,
your world, your rules, your ending.

Why this matters beyond ethics: copied work can't go in a portfolio. It can't be
submitted, sold, licensed, or shown to a studio. Original work can. Teaching a
kid this at twelve saves them a real problem at twenty-two.

Our students learn it in Week 1, before they design anything.

## Facebook rewrite

One of the first things we teach: **be inspired, don't copy.**

Most young artists start by redrawing characters they love. That's a normal
stage of learning and there's nothing wrong with it. But it becomes a problem
when nobody explains the difference between learning from something and taking
it.

Copied work can't be used. It can't go in a portfolio, be entered in a
competition, sold, or shown to a studio. Original work can do all of it.

We teach this in week one, before students design anything, so everything they
build over the six weeks genuinely belongs to them. That matters when a
portfolio is meant to open doors later.

# DAY 7 — World Building

**Format:** Carousel, 7 slides
**Teaching point:** A world is rules, not scenery.
**CTA:** Soft — comment

## Slide copy

**SLIDE 1 — Hook**
> ## A world isn't a background.
> It's a set of rules.

**SLIDE 2 — Tension**
> ## Beautiful setting, no rules.
> It looks incredible and the story goes nowhere.

**SLIDE 3 — Value**
> ## Rule one: what's possible here?
> If anyone can do anything, nothing your character does is impressive.
> Limits are what make an action mean something.

**SLIDE 4 — Value**
> ## Rule two: what does it cost?
> Every power needs a price. Energy, time, memory, a relationship.
> Free power is boring power.

**SLIDE 5 — Value**
> ## Rule three: who's in charge?
> Somebody runs this place. Somebody benefits.
> That's where your conflict is already hiding.

**SLIDE 6 — Payoff**
> ## Answer those three and the plot starts writing itself.
> Conflict comes from rules, not from scenery.

**SLIDE 7 — CTA**
> ## What's one rule in your world?
> Tell us in the comments.
> **@yourhandle**

## Instagram caption

A world isn't a background. It's a set of rules.

This is the single biggest jump we watch students make. They arrive able to
describe a place — floating city, neon streets, endless rain. Gorgeous, and
completely inert. Nothing can happen there yet, because nothing is decided.

Three questions turn a setting into a world:

**What's possible here?** If anyone can do anything, nothing anyone does is
impressive. Limits create stakes. Aoi's city runs on a network, and the network
has boundaries — that's why anything she does inside it counts.

**What does it cost?** Every ability needs a price. Energy, time, memory, a
relationship, a piece of who you are. Free power is boring power, every single
time.

**Who's in charge?** Somebody runs the place. Somebody benefits from it running
that way. Somebody doesn't. You just found your conflict without writing a
single plot point.

Answer those three and the story starts building itself. That's Week 3 in our
studio, and it's usually the week the ideas stop being pictures and start being
stories.

One rule from your world — comments.

## Facebook rewrite

Here's a creative-thinking exercise worth doing at your kitchen table.

Ask your young person about a world they've imagined, then ask three questions:
What's possible in this place? What does it cost to do those things? Who's in
charge?

Those three questions turn a drawing into a story. They're also genuine critical
thinking — establishing rules, tracing consequences, identifying who holds power
and who doesn't.

It's week three of our program, and it's usually the week we watch students shift
from making pictures to building something structured. The skill transfers well
past anime.

## Instagram caption

"Art isn't a real career."

Somebody says a version of this to a talented kid every day, and they're not
being cruel. They're picturing one job — a person drawing alone, hoping. They
don't know the map.

Here's some of what's actually on it:

**Character designer** — decides what everyone looks like before a frame gets
animated. Shape, palette, motif.
**Storyboard artist** — plans every shot before production spends money. Thinks
in camera angles.
**Background artist** — builds the world the characters move through.
**Color scripter** — controls the emotional temperature of every scene.
**Compositor** — assembles the final image from a dozen separate layers.
**Previs artist, layout artist, animation director, art director.**

And directly adjacent: game art, advertising, UX illustration, publishing,
brand design, film. The same core skills, many different doors.

Here's the part that matters most: nearly every one of these hires on a
**portfolio**. Not a degree. Not a connection. Work you can show. That's the
whole reason our six weeks ends with a portfolio instead of a certificate — a
certificate proves attendance, a portfolio proves capability.

Send this to somebody who needs to see the map.

# DAY 9 — AI Is a Tool With No Taste

**Format:** Reel / single image
**Teaching point:** Honest AI literacy. No overselling.
**CTA:** Medium

## Reel script

**Length:** 45 seconds

**Shot 1 (0–8s)** — Aoi, arms folded, direct address, unimpressed.
> "Let's be honest about AI, because most people selling it to you aren't."

**Shot 2 (9–20s)** — Medium, gesturing.
> "It's fast. It's genuinely useful. And it has no taste at all. It doesn't
> know what's good. It only knows what's common."

**Shot 3 (21–33s)** — Push in.
> "So when you use it without knowing design, you get the average of everything.
> Generic. Forgettable. The tool didn't fail. Nobody was steering."

**Shot 4 (34–45s)** — She turns toward the city.
> "That's why we teach the craft first and the tool second. The taste is the
> part that's yours."

**On-screen text:** "AI has no taste" / "it knows COMMON not GOOD" / "you steer"

**Generation prompt:** locked character block, then —
```
Composition: character standing with arms folded, level unimpressed expression,
direct address to camera. The violet tech light-bar panel on her left sleeve
pulses softly. Slow push-in to a medium close-up, then she turns to look out at

```

## Instagram caption

Honest version, because most people selling AI won't give you one.

AI is fast. It's genuinely useful. And it has no taste.

It doesn't know what's good. It knows what's *common* — it was built by learning
patterns across an enormous pile of existing work, and what it gives back is the
center of that pile. That's why AI art so often looks like AI art. You're
looking at an average.

Which is exactly why "anyone can make art now" is wrong. Anyone can generate an
image now. Those are different sentences.

The person who gets something good out of these tools is the person who already
knows what good looks like — who can look at a result and say the silhouette is
mushy, the palette has too many colors, the lighting contradicts the mood. That
judgment is craft. The tool cannot supply it.

So we teach it in that order. Students learn silhouette, palette, motif, story
structure, and shot planning — and then they use AI to move faster on decisions
they're already qualified to make.

The tool is the brush. Your kid is the artist. We're not confused about which
is which, and neither should anyone selling you a class be.

## Facebook rewrite

An honest word about AI, since it's the reason many parents hesitate.

looks the same.

That means the person who gets good results is the person who already understands

So we teach in that order. Students learn the fundamentals — silhouette, color,
story structure, shot planning — and then use AI to work faster on decisions
they're now qualified to make. They finish able to critique what a tool gives
them, which is a durable skill regardless of which tools exist in ten years.

We are not teaching your child to push a button. We're teaching them to direct.

# DAY 10 — You Don't Have to Draw

**Format:** Reel / single image
**Teaching point:** Removing the biggest objection in the room.
**CTA:** Medium

## Reel script

**Length:** 35 seconds

**Shot 1 (0–8s)** — Aoi seated at a glowing workstation, turning to camera.
> "'I can't draw.' That's the sentence that stops most people. Let it go."

**Shot 2 (9–19s)** — Over the shoulder at the screen.
> "Directors don't draw every frame. Producers don't draw at all. The person who
> decides what the show *is* often can't render it — and doesn't need to."

**Shot 3 (20–30s)** — Back to her, direct.
> "What you actually need is decisions. What does she look like? What does she
> want? What's in her way? That's the job."

**Shot 4 (31–35s)** — Small nod.
> "You can learn to render later. Start deciding now."

**On-screen text:** "'i can't draw'" / "you need DECISIONS not drawings" /
"start now"

**Generation prompt:** locked character block, then —
```
Composition: character seated at a glowing violet creator workstation with
floating holographic screens, turning in her chair to look at camera. Then an
over-the-shoulder shot showing the glowing screens. Warm violet studio lighting,
neon city visible through a window behind. Interior studio setting.
```

## Instagram caption

"I can't draw."

That sentence ends more creative lives than lack of talent ever has, and it's
built on a misunderstanding of how this work happens.

Animation is not one person drawing. It's a team of specialists, and many of the
most important people on it don't render anything. Directors make decisions.
Producers make decisions. Writers, showrunners, and art directors shape what the
thing *is* — and hand execution to people who specialize in execution.

What the work actually requires is **decisions**:

What does she look like, and why that instead of something else?
What does she want badly enough to act on?
Who's stopping her?
What does the audience feel in the first ten seconds?

That's the job. It's taste, structure, and judgment — and none of it requires a
steady hand.

Our students range from kids who draw constantly to kids who have never finished
a sketch. Both finish with a character, a world, a story, a storyboard, and an
animated scene. The tools handle rendering. We teach the deciding.

If your young person has ideas but says they can't draw — they were never
disqualified. Nobody told them the truth about the job.

## Facebook rewrite

"I can't draw" is the reason most families give for not signing up. It's worth
addressing directly.

Animation is a team craft, and many of the most important roles involve no
drawing at all. Directors, producers, writers, and art directors make decisions
about what a story is and how it should feel, and specialists execute.

Our program teaches the deciding: what a character looks like and why, what they
want, what stands in their way, what an audience should feel in the opening
seconds. Students use AI-assisted tools for the rendering.

Kids who draw constantly and kids who've never finished a sketch both finish
this program with a complete character, world, story, storyboard, and animated
scene.

If your child has ideas but thinks they're not "an art kid" — they were never
disqualified. They were just told the wrong thing about the job.
