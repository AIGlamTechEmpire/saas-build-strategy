# How To Design A Play

**The question:** *"How do I become the person who designs these instead of the person who runs them?"*

**The short answer:** you already did it once. You just did not number it.

---

## What a "play" actually is

A play is **one repeatable business motion, written down with enough constraints that an AI
cannot be lazy about it.**

That is the whole thing. Not a prompt. Not a tip. A *motion* — something you do over and over
in your business that produces the same kind of result each time.

The OPP plays take one motion — *get invited to speak to an audience someone else already
gathered* — and write it 50 different ways for 50 different kinds of organization.

---

## The anatomy — six parts, every one load-bearing

Take OPP #123 apart and this is what is inside it.

### 1. A named motion
> *"Find a Black employee network where my expertise creates a credible opportunity for
> practical AI education."*

Not a topic. A **motion**. Something that happens, with a beginning and an end.

### 2. A fixed sequence
> `ONE AUDIENCE → ONE DECISION MAKER → ONE OFFER → EMAIL SENT`

Four steps, in order, no skipping. **The order is the product.** This is the same discipline as
the Revenue Build Order and the same as your own Bleeding → Feeding → Needing.

### 3. Hard constraints that stop the AI being generic
This is the part most people miss, and it is where the quality actually comes from:

| Constraint in the play | What it prevents |
|---|---|
| *"Do NOT give me a long list. Select the ONE strongest target."* | The AI hedging with 10 options so you have to decide |
| *"NEVER invent, infer, guess, or construct contact information."* | Fabricated emails that burn your reputation |
| *"150 words or less."* | A wall of text nobody reads |
| *"Do not lead with pricing."* | Closing before there is a conversation |
| *"Do not give me a generic 'I am an AI consultant' pitch."* | The exact output an unconstrained prompt produces |

**Every constraint is a mistake somebody already made.** That is where they come from. You do not
invent constraints — you harvest them from things that went wrong.

### 4. A defined output format
The play says exactly what comes back: organization, decision maker, contact method, training
offered, date contacted, follow-up date, next action.

**If the output format is not specified, you get prose. Prose does not go in a tracker.**

### 5. A completion condition
> *"MY GOAL TODAY IS NOT RESEARCH. MY GOAL IS AN EMAIL SENT. Do not consider this exercise
> complete until I confirm the outreach was sent."*

This is the sharpest line in the entire document. Without it, "research a target" feels like
progress and nothing ships. **Name what done means, and make done a verb, not a document.**

### 6. A number
`#123`. Not a name — a number.

Numbering turns tips into a **library**. A library can be worked through, delegated, tracked,
sold, and handed to an agent. Fifty numbered plays is a product. Fifty good ideas is a folder.

## You already built one

Your Bleeding / Feeding / Needing method has every single part except the number:

| Part | Yours |
|---|---|
| Named motion | Diagnose where a business is losing time and money, and what to do in 30 days |
| Fixed sequence | Conversation → Brain → Bleed → Feed → Need → SOPs → Automate → AI last |
| Hard constraints | *AI is the last phase.* *Human conversation first.* *No SOP, no automation.* |
| Output format | The Opportunity Map, scored and ranked |
| Completion condition | **⚠ MISSING — define this** |
| Number | **⚠ MISSING — number them** |

**Two gaps. Both fixable this week.**

Your completion condition should be a verb, the way Alicia's is. Something like:
*"This is not complete until one task has been moved from Doing to Delegating and the client
has the hours back."* Not a report. A moved task.

## How to write your first ten

Do not start from a blank page. Start from what you already repeat.

**Step 1 — list the motions you already do.** Not topics. Things you *do*, repeatedly, that
produce a result. Ten minutes, be blunt:
- Diagnose where a business is bleeding
- Turn a bleed into an SOP
- Turn an SOP into an automation
- Work a 12-month goal back into a 30-day plan
- Decide whether a task should be automated or just stopped

**Step 2 — for each one, write the fixed sequence.** Three to five steps. If you cannot write
the sequence, it is not a play yet — it is a topic.

**Step 3 — write the constraints from your scar tissue.** For each play ask: *what does someone
get wrong every single time?* That answer becomes a "do NOT" line. This is where your years
of experience actually get encoded, and it is the part nobody can copy from watching you.

**Step 4 — define the output format.** What comes back, in what shape, so it can go in a tracker.

**Step 5 — write the completion condition.** A verb. Something that either happened or did not.

**Step 6 — number it.** `PBR #001`. Pinky Brain Revenue, play one.

## Where this pattern comes from

Two honest parts to this answer.

**I do not know Alicia Lyttle's specific sources**, and I am not going to guess at them.

**But the underlying strategy is old and well documented.** "Other People's People" is a
repackaging of what direct-response marketers have called **host-beneficiary** relationships for
decades — Jay Abraham built much of his career on it in the 1980s and 90s. The core move is the
same: rather than build an audience, find someone who already has your audience and has a reason
to introduce you to them. Joint ventures, affiliate deals, guest lectures, association speaking
and podcast tours are all versions of it.

**So what is actually new here is not the strategy. It is the packaging.**

The strategy is decades old. Turning it into 50 numbered, constrained, AI-executable prompts
with a completion condition — that is the innovation, and that is the part you can do too.

**Which means you do not need to invent new business strategy.** You need to take strategy that
already works — including your own, which is genuinely yours — and package it as constrained,
numbered plays.

That is the whole trade.

## Where to mine raw material

If you want more plays, the source material is the direct-response canon, because those people
wrote down repeatable motions obsessively:

- **Jay Abraham** — host-beneficiary, joint ventures, borrowed audiences
- **Dan Kennedy** — direct response offers and deadline mechanics
- **Alex Hormozi** — offer construction and value stacking
- **Chet Holmes** — the Dream 100 (pick 100 target organizations and work them relentlessly — an
  extremely close cousin of the OPP plays)

**Chet Holmes' Dream 100 is worth your time specifically.** It is the same shape as OPP: a finite
named list of target organizations, worked repeatedly rather than sprayed. Read that, and the
structure of what Alicia built will be obvious to you.

## The uncomfortable part

Designing plays is a **high-value, low-urgency** activity. It never feels like today's fire.

You are working 450 hours a month and this will always lose to something louder. So put a
constraint on it, the same way a play does:

**One play per week. Written down. Numbered. Non-negotiable.**

Fifty weeks from now you own a library. And a library is an asset — it can be taught, licensed,
handed to an agent, or sold. Fifty good ideas in your head is not an asset, and it does not
survive you being tired.
