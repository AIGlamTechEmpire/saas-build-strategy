# CHAPTER ONE
## The Only Woman in the Engineering Program

Okay, so let me put you in the room.

It is early. The building still has that cold morning air in it, the kind that has been sitting all night with nobody in the halls. I have my bag on my shoulder. I have my notebook. I have my pen, and honey, I had the right pen, because I take my notes seriously.

And I walk through that door and I do what every woman reading this book has done at some point in her life without ever deciding to do it.

I counted.

Men. Men. Men. Young men, older men, a man with his feet already up, a man with three energy drinks lined up on the desk like he was planning a war. And then there was me.

One.

Now let me tell you what I did *not* do, because I need this straight from the jump.

I did not shrink. I did not sit in the back. I did not get quiet and hope nobody noticed me. I was not standing in that doorway asking myself whether I belonged in an engineering program.

I knew I belonged in an engineering program.

My father raised a woman with her chin up. That man did not hand me self-doubt as a starter kit. And beyond what he gave me, I had *evidence*. I had been invited into engineering programs. I had been picked for technology summer camps — picked, out of a pile of other students, because of the work I did. Somebody looked at my paper and said, "Her. Send her."

So when I counted that room and came up with one woman, I did not read that as a warning.

I read it as a fact about the room.

And I sat down in the front, opened my notebook, and got ready to work.

That is the woman I need you to see. Because the story I am about to tell you is not a story about a scared woman. It is a story about a prepared woman running straight into a wall that had nothing to do with her ability — and getting *angry* about it.

---

There was a piece of equipment on the table.

It had a screen. It had a whole row of knobs. It had cables coming off of it. It had that look serious machines have — nobody built it to be pretty, somebody built it to do a job.

An oscilloscope.

And the assignment was this: we were required to write out the engineering plans for it.

Write out the plans. For a machine I had never laid eyes on in my life.

I had never seen one. Not in the camps, not in a classroom, not on a table, not in anybody's hands. I did not know what it did. I did not know what it measured. I did not know what problem it existed to solve or why an engineer would ever need one.

I knew it had knobs. That was my entire inventory.

And that was fine. That is what school is *for*. You are supposed to walk in not knowing and walk out knowing. That is the whole transaction.

So the instructor starts explaining.

And I sit there with my good pen, listening hard, waiting for the sentence that opens the door.

And it does not come.

---

So I raised my hand.

I asked him a real question. Not a vague question. Not a "can you go over that again" question. I asked him what that machine actually *was*. What does it measure? What is it supposed to do? How does this thing connect to the electrical engineering theory we have been sitting here learning?

Four honest questions. Any one of them would have unlocked it for me.

And he answered.

By saying the exact same thing he had just said.

Louder.

Now, I want you to understand something about me. I am not a woman who takes one no and folds up. So I raised my hand again.

Same thing. Same words, same order, more volume, faster.

I raised my hand *again*.

I asked it a different way, because I thought maybe the problem was how I had worded it. Maybe if I came at it from another direction, he would come at it from another direction.

He did not. He came back with the same explanation, cooked at a higher temperature.

And somewhere around that third or fourth time, something in me clicked over.

And it was not shame.

I want to be very clear, because this is where most books about women in these rooms get it wrong, and it is the exact place I refuse to be gotten wrong.

I was not embarrassed. I was not questioning myself. I did not sit in that seat and think, *maybe I am not good at this.*

I was hot.

I was *angry*. I was angry that I had asked a serious question four different ways and gotten the same recording played back at me four times. I was angry that a man standing in front of a room full of adults did not have a second way to explain his own subject. I was angry that everybody around me was nodding and I could not figure out what they were nodding at.

And underneath the anger — and this is the part that saved me — was determination.

Because I sat there and I thought: *Fine. If you cannot explain this to me, I will go find somebody who can. Or I will go find out myself. But I am not leaving here not knowing.*

That was not fear. That was a decision.

---

Now here is what took me longer to figure out, and it is the real lesson of this chapter.

He was not withholding it from me.

He genuinely did not have another way to say it.

That man had one explanation. One. It was the explanation that had been given to him, in the language it had been given to him, and it had worked on him, so as far as he was concerned it was *the* explanation. When it did not land on me, he did not think, "Let me find another door." He thought, "She must not have heard me."

So he turned up the volume.

And **saying it louder does not make it clearer.**

**Volume is not clarity.**

Write that down somewhere, because you are going to meet forty more of these people in your life. In classrooms, in trainings, in onboarding, in that thirty-five-minute demo where the man clicks so fast you cannot see where his mouse went. A person with one explanation will always assume the failure is in your ears.

It is not in your ears. It is in his inventory.

**Do not mistake poor delivery for your inability.**

---

And there was something else happening in that room that I did not have words for until years later, when I read a book called *Games Mother Never Taught You*.

That book named something I had been feeling my whole professional life.

A lot of these rooms do not just teach the subject. They teach the subject *through a language*. And that language is usually sports, or the military, or some version of a game the boys in the room were raised inside of.

You will hear it. "Think of it like a quarterback reading the field." "You are running interference here." "This is your first line of defense." "Now we are going to run the play." "You have to know when to punt."

And every man in that room nods, because he has been swimming in that water since he was six years old. Somebody put him on a team. Somebody taught him the positions. Somebody explained strategy to him at a dinner table before he could read.

Nobody sat me down and taught me the plays.

Now, understand what I am saying and what I am not saying. I am not saying women cannot understand sports. Plenty of us can run circles around these men on a football conversation. I am saying that when a room builds its *teaching* on top of a game, and you were never taught that game, you are now doing two jobs at once. You are trying to learn the actual material *and* translate the metaphor at the same time — while everybody else is doing one job.

And when you fall behind doing two jobs, they write it down as though you failed at one.

That is not an intelligence gap. That is a translation tax.

**Do not mistake a game you were never taught for a game you cannot learn.**

The material was never the problem. The material was electrical engineering, and I loved electrical engineering. The problem was that the material was wrapped in a package addressed to somebody else, and I was expected to unwrap it in real time without ever being handed the key.

---

Which brings me to the sentence that ended up shaping this entire book.

I was already in the room.

Do you understand what I am saying? Nobody kept me out. I got admitted. I registered. I paid. I sat in the front row with my notebook open and my hand up. Every door between me and that education was standing wide open.

And I still could not get in.

**Access is not just whether the door is open. Access is whether the explanation lets you enter.**

That is the whole thing right there. That is the sentence I wish somebody had handed me at twenty-five, at thirty-five, at every job I ever had where I sat in a training that taught me nothing and then got measured as though I had been taught.

They will point at the open door as proof that you have access. "The program is open to everybody." "Anybody can sign up." "The training was available to the whole team."

And meanwhile the explanation is a locked room inside the open building.

---

And that is where the seed of the rest of my life got planted, sitting in that chair, mad.

Because I started thinking: somebody built this. Somebody decided what would be taught, and in what order, and in whose language, and at what speed, and who would be standing at the front, and what happens when a woman raises her hand four times.

That was a design. A person made those choices.

And if a person made those choices, a person could make different ones.

Everybody wants to talk about getting a seat at the table. Build your own table, they say. But I have sat at tables, and I want to tell you what I learned: **the table is still inside somebody else's building.** They own the door. They own the lights. They decide what gets taught on every floor and who gets to walk up to the next one and who gets a key.

A chair is not ownership. A chair is permission.

So I stopped wanting a chair, and I started wanting a building. A whole building, designed by a woman who has been the only one in the room and did not like how it was run. A building where a woman can learn, ask her question four different ways and get four different answers, build proof that she can hold in her hands, and walk out with a key.

That is not a metaphor I invented at a desk. That is a thing I started wanting while I was angry in an engineering class.

---

Now let me talk to you directly, because I did not write this chapter so you could hear about a room I sat in a long time ago.

Some of you reading this are exactly where I was. You are qualified. You have the background, the grades, the years, the receipts. And you are sitting in a training or a class or a meeting where a man is explaining something in one voice at one speed, and you are not getting it, and you are furious about it — because you know good and well that you are not the problem.

You are right. You are not the problem.

And some of you are somewhere else entirely, and I want to speak to you too, plainly, because your experience is not mine and I am not going to pretend it is.

Some of you never had my start. Nobody picked you for a camp. Nobody told you that you were technical. You feel intimidated by things other people call basic — the software at work, the AI everybody keeps talking about, the dashboard, the platform, the blinking cursor in that little box. And every time you get near it, something in your chest says *this is not for me.*

I did not carry that voice, and I am not going to stand here and act like I did. My father did not leave room in me for it.

But I have watched it in women I love. I have watched brilliant women — women who run households on budgets that should not work, women who manage people who do not want to be managed, women who hold a family and a job and a church and somebody else's crisis all in the same week without dropping any of it — freeze up in front of a screen and decide something ugly about themselves in about four seconds.

And I want to tell you what I know from the other side of it.

That sentence in your chest is not a measurement. Nobody tested you. Nobody ran the numbers on your mind and came back with a result. That sentence was *installed*. Somebody rushed you one time. Somebody sighed at your question one time. Somebody explained a thing in a language you were never taught and then looked at you like you were slow.

And you have been carrying their bad teaching around like it was a diagnosis.

**Do not mistake exclusion for inability.**

---

I will tell you how my story ended, and I am not going to dress it up.

That room never changed. That instructor never found a second explanation. Nobody came and found me and said, "Let me show you this a different way." That is not what happened, and honestly, that is not usually what happens.

What changed is that I stopped waiting for him to become a better teacher.

I went and got my hands on that machine. Because that is who I have always been — I am a tinkerer. I have been a tinkerer since I was a girl. And a tinkerer does not learn a machine by being told about it in somebody else's metaphor. A tinkerer learns a machine by turning the knob and watching what moves.

So I turned the knobs. I watched the screen. I asked what happens if I do this, and what breaks if I do that, and why on earth would somebody design it this way instead of that way. And I went and found other explanations — other books, other people, other words for the same thing — until I found the one that opened it.

And you know what an oscilloscope actually is?

It is a machine that draws a picture of electricity so a human being can look at it and see what it is doing.

That is it. One sentence. One honest sentence and the whole thing would have opened up for me in the first ten minutes of that class.

He never gave me that sentence. Somebody else did. And the day I got it, everything he had been shouting at me for weeks suddenly made perfect sense — same woman, same mind, same machine.

Different delivery.

That was the first time in my life I understood something I have built everything on since.

Access is not given. Access is designed. And if nobody has designed it for you, you can go get it yourself — and then, when you are strong enough, you can turn around and design it for somebody else.

That is the whole book. That is what we are doing here.

## REFLECTION EXERCISE 1
### Was I Incapable, or Was This a Delivery and Access Problem?

Do this one first. Every woman reading this book should do this one, whether or not you ever doubted yourself.

Get paper. Not your phone. Paper and a pen you actually like.

**One.** Pick one specific time you did not understand something technical. A class, a training, a demo, a meeting, a manual, a conversation. Write down where you were and what was being explained.

**Two.** Now run the diagnosis. Circle every one that was true:

- The words were never defined for me.
- It moved faster than a human being could follow.
- I was told *how* to click it, but never *what it is* or *why it works.*
- I had never physically seen or touched the thing before.
- The explanation used sports, military, or "guy" analogies I was never taught.
- I asked, and I got the same answer again — just louder.
- The example used was from a world I have never lived in.
- Everybody else had already been introduced to it, and nobody said so out loud.
- I got lost at one specific point, and everything after that was gone.

**Three.** Count your circles. Every single line on that list is a *delivery* problem or an *access* problem. Not one of them is a mind problem.

**Four.** Now write the four questions I asked in that engineering class, filled in for your own situation:

- What is this thing, in one plain sentence?
- What does it actually do or measure?
- What problem does it exist to solve?
- How does it connect to what I already know?

If nobody ever answered those four questions for you, you were never taught. You were *talked at.*

**Five.** Finish this sentence in writing:

*The door was open, but the explanation did not let me in. What I actually needed was ____________.*

Be specific. A demonstration. My hands on it. The why before the how. A different teacher. Somebody to define one word. An example from my own life instead of a football field.

**Six.** Write this one down and keep it:

*Access is not just whether the door is open. Access is whether the explanation lets me enter. And I am allowed to go find an explanation that does.*

## REFLECTION EXERCISE 2
### "Maybe I Am Not Good at This"

This one is for the woman who took it personally. If that sentence has ever lived in your chest, this exercise is for you, and I want you to do it slowly.

**One.** Write the sentence down exactly the way it sounds in your head. *Maybe I am not good at this. Maybe I am too old for this. Maybe this is not for me. Maybe I am just not a tech person.*

Whatever the words are, put them on paper. You have never once looked at them outside of your own head.

**Two.** Now trace it back. Where did that sentence come from? Somebody said something. Somebody sighed. Somebody rushed you. Somebody laughed. A teacher, a boss, a coworker, a husband, a brother, a classmate, a comment somebody made twenty years ago that they have completely forgotten and you have not.

Write down who. Write down when. Write down what was actually said.

**Three.** Ask the hard question: *Was that person ever qualified to make that judgment about my mind?*

Usually you will find out it was one frustrated person having one bad moment — and you took their bad moment and made it your identity.

**Four.** Now bring evidence, because you have some and you have never used it in your own defense. Write down three hard things you have already learned in your life. A job, a system, a diagnosis, a language, a route, a recipe, a program, a person. Things you did not know and now know cold.

Look at that list. That is a woman who can learn.

**Five.** Now replace the sentence. Cross out the old one and write the accurate one:

*I was not incapable. I was ____________.*

Untaught. Unintroduced. Rushed. Skipped. Talked at instead of taught. Given one explanation by a person who only had one. Handed a game nobody ever taught me the rules to.

Use the true word.

**Six.** Last thing. Write this and put it where you will see it this week:

*Saying it louder does not make it clearer. Volume is not clarity. I do not need it repeated. I need it explained differently — and I am allowed to go get that.*

---

That is the first move, and it is the most important one in this whole book.

You are not going to fix this by feeling better about yourself. You are going to fix it by getting an accurate diagnosis. Because a woman who believes she is incapable will quit.

But a woman who knows she was never taught?

She goes and gets taught.

And then, when she is strong enough, she builds a place where the next woman does not have to raise her hand four times.
